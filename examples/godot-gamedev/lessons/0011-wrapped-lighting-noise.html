Wrapped Lighting & Noise Bias

Lesson 11 · Per-Material Toon Shading (MKToon) · ~9 min read
Win: You can soften the shadow terminator with wrapped lighting and add organic edge variation with noise bias — and explain why full wrap eliminates banding.
After this lesson: Your toon shader's shadow boundaries feel hand-painted rather than geometrically perfect. Two independent techniques — wrapped lighting and noise perturbation — break the "CG look" with minimal code.

The Problem: Geometric Perfection Looks CG

← Previous: Lesson 10: Gooch Shading

Your Gooch shader produces beautiful warm/cool shadows — but look at where those shadows fall. On a sphere, the shadow boundary is a perfect circle. On a cube face, it's a razor-straight line. This geometric perfection is a dead giveaway that a computer rendered this. Real illustrated or painted work has two qualities our shader currently lacks:

  • Softer terminators — shadow doesn't cut off abruptly at the 90° angle to the light. Light wraps slightly past the geometric edge.
  • Irregular boundaries — shadow edges waver and wobble organically, as if brushed by hand rather than computed by dot product.

We'll add both in this lesson, independently, with about 5 lines of code total.

Wrapped Lighting: Softening the Terminator

Standard Lambert lighting drops to zero at the geometric terminator — the exact point where the surface faces 90° away from the light (NdotL = 0). In reality, light scatters and bounces, so surfaces don't go fully dark at this angle. Valve solved this for Half-Life (1998) with what they called "Half Lambert":

// Wrapped lighting: raise the floor of NdotL (Half Lambert variant)
float wrap = clamp(wrapped_lighting * wrapped_lighting_scale, 0.0, 1.0);
float wrapped = NdotL * (1.0 - wrap) + wrap;

The formula NdotL * (1.0 - wrap) + wrap compresses the lighting range. Instead of [0, 1], the output becomes [wrap, 1]. At the default wrap = 0.35:

  • A surface facing directly away from light (NdotL = 0) → output = 0.35 (not pure dark)
  • A surface facing directly at the light (NdotL = 1) → output = 1.0 (unchanged)
  • The shadow "wraps" past the 90° terminator — more of the surface receives some light
New concept — wrapped lighting: A bias on NdotL that extends illumination past the geometric terminator. Valve's Half Lambert (1998) is the canonical version. Not physically accurate — it violates energy conservation — but for toon shading we're quantizing the result anyway, so conservation doesn't matter. What matters is: the shadow boundary shifts, and more of the surface participates in the banding.

Why Two Parameters?

The effective wrap is wrapped_lighting × wrapped_lighting_scale. Why not a single wrap uniform?

Separation of concerns:

  • wrapped_lighting (default 1.0) — a global enable/amount. At 0, no wrap at all (standard Lambert). At 1, the wrap system is fully active.
  • wrapped_lighting_scale (default 0.35) — how much the wrap contributes to the final centering. Controls the actual visual strength.

This matters because wrap can blow out band contrast if set too high. You want the terminator to soften without the entire surface becoming uniformly lit. The scale keeps wrap restrained: full half-Lambert wrapping (scale = 1.0) would compress NdotL's range so much that bands become invisible. The 0.35 default was empirically tuned across 468 materials in a shipped game — it softens without flattening.

Noise Bias: Organic Shadow Edges

Wrapped lighting softens the terminator but keeps it geometrically smooth. For truly organic-looking shadows, we perturb the band threshold with a noise texture:

// Noise bias: per-pixel threshold perturbation for organic edges
float noise_bias = 0.0;
if (use_noise_map) {
    noise_bias = (texture(noise_map, UV * noise_scale).r - 0.5) * noise_strength;
}

Then add noise_bias to the centering formula:

// Center around threshold (with wrap and noise folded in)
float centered = clamp(wrapped - light_threshold - diffuse_threshold_offset + 0.5 + noise_bias, 0.0, 1.0);

The mechanism: sample a grayscale texture, center it around zero (- 0.5), scale by strength, and add to the centering value. This creates a small signed offset (±0.02 at default strength) that locally shifts where each band boundary falls.

New concept — noise bias: A per-pixel offset to the banding threshold, sampled from a grayscale texture. It doesn't change brightness — it changes WHERE band transitions occur. The effect: shadow edges become wavy, irregular, organic. Like the difference between a ruler-drawn line and a brush stroke.

Noise Should Be Felt, Not Seen

The noise_strength range is capped at 0.25 for a reason. Since the banding operates on a [0, 1] scale, even a small noise offset creates visible edge variation. The defaults:

StrengthEffect range (±)Visual character
0.02±0.01Barely perceptible — breaks mathematical perfection without being obvious
0.04 (default)±0.02Subtle organic feel. You notice it's not CG but can't point to why
0.10±0.05Painterly — clearly irregular edges, watercolor character
0.25±0.125Aggressive — hatched/textured feel, breaks band coherence at high band counts

At low band counts (1–2), noise is nearly invisible because there are few edges to perturb. At high band counts (6+), each band is narrow enough that even subtle noise creates visible rippling along every boundary. Match your noise strength to your band count.

Choosing a Noise Texture

The shader samples one channel (red) from whatever texture you assign. Different textures produce different artistic characters:

  • Perlin/Simplex noise — smooth organic variation. The standard choice. Creates gentle rolling edges.
  • Cellular/Worley noise — produces hard-edged patches. Shadow boundaries break into distinct regions — a cracked-paint or cobblestone feel.
  • Hand-drawn texture — scan a brushstroke or ink texture. Produces truly unique, non-repeating character.
  • Value noise — simpler than Perlin, blockier at low resolution. Good for pixel-art adjacent styles.

The noise_scale uniform tiles the texture across UVs. Higher values = smaller noise features = more fine-grained edge variation. A scale of 1.0 means the texture maps 1:1 with the mesh's UV layout.

The Complete Shader

Building on Lesson 10's Gooch shader, the additions are the wrapped lighting formula (before centering) and the noise bias (added to centering). Everything else — banding, smoothness, scale, Gooch — remains unchanged:

shader_type spatial;
render_mode specular_disabled;

// === Albedo ===
uniform vec4 albedo_color : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform sampler2D albedo_texture : source_color;
uniform bool use_albedo_texture = false;

// === Toon Banding Controls ===
uniform float light_bands : hint_range(1, 9, 1) = 4.0;
uniform float light_bands_scale : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;
uniform float light_threshold : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;
uniform float diffuse_threshold_offset : hint_range(-1, 1) = 0.0;
uniform float diffuse_smoothness : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.0;

// === Wrapped Lighting ===
uniform float wrapped_lighting : hint_range(0, 1) = 1.0;
uniform float wrapped_lighting_scale : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.35;

// === Noise Map ===
uniform bool use_noise_map = false;
uniform sampler2D noise_map;
uniform float noise_strength : hint_range(0.0, 0.25) = 0.04;
uniform float noise_scale : hint_range(0.1, 10.0) = 1.0;

// === Gooch Shading ===
uniform vec4 gooch_bright_color : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform vec4 gooch_dark_color : source_color = vec4(0.2, 0.25, 0.35, 1.0);
uniform float gooch_ramp_intensity : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;

void fragment() {
    vec3 col = albedo_color.rgb;
    if (use_albedo_texture) {
        col *= texture(albedo_texture, UV).rgb;
    }
    ALBEDO = col;
}

void light() {
    float NdotL = clamp(dot(NORMAL, LIGHT), 0.0, 1.0);

    // Wrapped lighting: raise the floor of NdotL (Half Lambert variant)
    float wrap = clamp(wrapped_lighting * wrapped_lighting_scale, 0.0, 1.0);
    float wrapped = NdotL * (1.0 - wrap) + wrap;

    // Noise bias: per-pixel threshold perturbation for organic edges
    float noise_bias = 0.0;
    if (use_noise_map) {
        noise_bias = (texture(noise_map, UV * noise_scale).r - 0.5) * noise_strength;
    }

    // Center around threshold (with wrap and noise folded in)
    float centered = clamp(wrapped - light_threshold - diffuse_threshold_offset + 0.5 + noise_bias, 0.0, 1.0);

    // Band quantization
    float banded = floor(centered * light_bands) / max(light_bands - 1.0, 1.0);

    // Smoothness blend
    float lit_factor = mix(banded, centered, diffuse_smoothness);

    // Scale
    lit_factor = 0.5 + (lit_factor - 0.5) * light_bands_scale;

    // Gooch shading
    vec3 shadow_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_dark_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
    vec3 lit_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_bright_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
    vec3 toon_color = mix(shadow_tint, lit_tint, lit_factor);

    DIFFUSE_LIGHT = max(DIFFUSE_LIGHT, toon_color * LIGHT_COLOR * ATTENUATION);
}

Check Your Understanding

Exercise: A colleague sets wrapped_lighting = 1.0 and wrapped_lighting_scale = 1.0 expecting "maximum shadow softness." Instead, their mesh becomes uniformly bright with no visible toon bands — just flat color with Gooch tinting. They expected softer band transitions, not the absence of bands. Explain what happened.

Hint

Plug wrap = 1.0 into the formula NdotL * (1.0 - wrap) + wrap. What does NdotL get multiplied by? What's the output regardless of surface angle?

Answer

With wrap = 1.0: NdotL * (1.0 - 1.0) + 1.0 = NdotL * 0 + 1.0 = 1.0 for every pixel. The entire surface reports "fully lit" regardless of its angle to the light. When wrapped is constant (1.0), centered is constant, banded is constant — so there's nothing to quantize into bands. The shader still applies Gooch tinting (since lit_factor becomes a constant near 1.0), but the spatial variation that makes bands visible is gone.

For soft-but-visible banding, use wrapped_lighting_scale in the 0.3–0.5 range. This compresses the NdotL range to [0.3–0.5, 1.0] — enough softening to feel natural without eliminating the variation that banding needs. The 0.35 default was tuned across hundreds of materials in a shipped game.
