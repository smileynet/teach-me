What Makes a Texture Toon-Unfriendly?

Lesson 15 · Blender to Godot Texture Prep · ~12 min read
Win: You can look at any PBR texture set and predict which channels will fight toon shading, decide what to keep, discard, or repurpose, and explain why quantization amplifies texture noise.
After this lesson: PBR textures encode continuous properties; toon shaders discretize them. Continuous detail plus discrete shading equals amplified noise — so the fix is to simplify the texture before it reaches the shader, not to fight the shader.

The Problem

Here are two renders of the same barrel, under the same toon shader, with the same lighting and the same mesh. The only difference is the texture.

The render on the right isn't wrong because the shader is bad. It's wrong because photoreal PBR textures carry exactly the kind of detail that toon shading is designed to throw away — and when you quantize that detail instead of discarding it, you amplify it into noise. This lesson is about learning to see that conflict before you try to fix it. No Blender yet; just diagnosis.

Quantization amplifies noise

A toon shader takes the continuous lighting value at each pixel — how directly that point faces the light, a smooth number from 0 to 1 — and snaps it into a handful of discrete bands. That snap is a step function: everything between two thresholds collapses to one flat tone.

Feed a smooth signal through that step function and you get clean, wide bands. Feed a noisy signal through the same step function and every little wiggle that happens to straddle a threshold flips between bands — turning fine detail into speckle. The step function doesn't smooth noise; it hardens it into visible edges.

New concept — quantization: collapsing a continuous range of values into a small set of discrete steps. Toon shading quantizes lighting on purpose — that's what makes the flat cel look. The catch: quantization is indifferent to where the input came from. Detail you wanted (broad form) and detail you didn't (surface grain) get the same hard treatment.

The three enemies

Let's isolate the sources of trouble by toggling one texture channel at a time on the barrel. Start from the clean reference, add one thing, observe what breaks.

Start: flat color only

Three clean bands, sweeping across the curved face. The side-raking light puts the shadow terminator across the front, so the lighting value sweeps the full range and each band owns a wide, readable stripe. This is the target look — hold it in mind as the baseline.

+ albedo texture

Turn on the diffuse (albedo) texture and the bands vanish into a storm of rust, scratches, weld seams, and a printed hazard label. The bands are technically still there — but the high-frequency color detail is louder than the lighting. This is the “busy” look: not stylized, not realistic, just noisy.

+ normal map

Now go back to flat color but enable the normal map. The bands return — but look at the boundary between them. Instead of a clean line it's a jagged, chattering sawtooth. The normal map perturbs the surface direction pixel-by-pixel, so pixels near a band threshold flip back and forth across it. That's the quantization-amplifies-noise mechanism from Section 1, made literal: continuous normal detail pushed through the step function becomes shadow speckle.

Both together

Both problems at once: bands buried under albedo detail and a speckled terminator from the normal map. A realistic model with broken lighting — the render you almost never want.

When to keep vs discard the normal map:
  • Keep & simplify — when the mesh has broad forms you actually want the shader to shade (folds in cloth, large dents). Flatten the fine detail, keep the big shapes.
  • Discard entirely — when the silhouette already communicates the form (a barrel's rings, a hard-surface prop). The normal map only adds speckle here.
Default: disable the normal map first. Add it back only if the result looks too flat — and if you do, flatten its fine detail before you trust it.

Channel triage

A typical PBR asset ships more than a diffuse map. The roughness, metallic, and ambient-occlusion channels are often packed into a single ARM texture (Ambient occlusion / Roughness / Metallic, one per color channel). Before you touch Blender, decide the fate of each channel.

The two discards are free: our shader runs with specular turned off, so roughness and metallic have nothing to plug into — there's no specular lobe for them to modulate. The AO channel is the interesting one. It already encodes where a surface is self-shadowed, which is exactly the kind of spatial bias a toon shader can use to nudge where the shadow boundary falls.

New concept — threshold_map: a texture that biases, per pixel, where the shadow boundary lands. Darker pixels pull the terminator earlier (more shadow); lighter pixels push it later. The AO channel already carries this spatial relationship, so it can be repurposed as a threshold map instead of thrown away. You'll author one later in this track.

How the pros do it

Alternative: AAA toon games (Guilty Gear, Genshin Impact) don't convert PBR textures at all — they hand-paint every texture for non-photoreal rendering from scratch, including custom control maps with per-pixel shadow and highlight authoring. That's the highest-quality path and the most expensive.

When to use which:
  • Hand-paint from scratch — choose when the art style is the product (a character-action or fighting game where every silhouette is scrutinized) and you have artist time to author ILM/shadow maps per asset.
  • Simplify existing PBR (this track) — choose when you're starting from free or purchased PBR assets, want to keep dynamic lighting, and need a clean result without a texture artist on payroll.
Default: simplify PBR unless the toon look is your game's headline feature — hand-painting is a large, ongoing per-asset cost you take on deliberately, not by default.

Check Your Understanding

Exercise: A colleague is prepping a PBR castle-wall asset for the same toon shader. They assign the albedo texture and disable the normal map. “The normal map was causing shadow speckle, so I removed it,” they explain. But the toon bands still look noisy and the areas that should be flat aren't flat. Why? What is the actual remaining source of the noise, and what would you recommend they do about it?

Hint

The normal map is only one of the three enemies. Which channel did they leave fully enabled and untouched? Re-read Section 1: what kinds of continuous input get amplified by quantization?

Answer

Disabling the normal map removes the speckled terminator, but it does nothing about the albedo texture, which they left fully enabled. A photoreal albedo map is itself a continuous, high-frequency signal — stone grain, mortar lines, moss, dirt, color variation. When the toon shader quantizes lighting over that busy color, the detail reads as noise and the “flat” areas are never actually flat, because the color underneath them isn't flat.

The misconception is that normal maps are the only problem. In fact any continuous, high-frequency channel fights toon shading — and albedo is usually the loudest one. The fix isn't to delete the albedo (you need base color) but to simplify it: posterize it to a few discrete colors and snap it to a limited palette so it stops carrying detail the shader will amplify. That's the next lesson.
