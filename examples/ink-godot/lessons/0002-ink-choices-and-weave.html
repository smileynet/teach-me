Choices, Stitches & Weave

Lesson 2 · Ink + Godot · ~12 min read
Win: You can write ink stories where players make choices that diverge briefly then reconverge via gathers — using stitches to organize sub-scenes and bracket syntax to control what text appears where.
After this lesson: You can build a multi-scene ink story with player choices (once-only and sticky), sub-sections within knots, and the gather pattern that brings branches back together without spaghetti diverts.

Your First Choice

In lesson 01, choices were brief — pick a path, divert to a knot. Now we'll explore what choices can really do. The simplest choice uses *:

The path splits ahead.
* You take the left fork.
  The trail winds through dense ferns.
* You take the right fork.
  Sunlight breaks through the canopy.

After the player picks, flow continues below the chosen option. The text of the choice becomes part of the output — "You take the left fork." appears in the story. Both options lead to whatever comes next, forming a funnel: brief divergence, immediate convergence.

New concept — choices aren't always branches: Not every choice needs to send the player down a fundamentally different path. Many choices are "micro-decisions" — they give the reader agency and voice without splitting the narrative. The funnel pattern (choices that reconverge immediately) is the workhorse of interactive dialogue.

Bracket Tricks: Controlling What Shows

Choice text often reads awkwardly in the output. "You take the left fork." works as a choice label, but you might want the output to say something different. Square brackets split choice text into three zones:

"What do you make of it?" she asked.
* "Fascinating[."]," I replied. "Truly fascinating."
* "I'm not sure[."] what to think," I admitted.
* "Honestly[?"] I couldn't care less."

The bracket rule: text before [ shows in both the choice and the output. Text inside [] shows only in the choice. Text after ] shows only in the output. This lets you write natural dialogue where the choice preview is concise but the spoken line is complete:

// Choice displays: "Fascinating."
// Output reads:   "Fascinating," I replied. "Truly fascinating."

To suppress choice text entirely — showing nothing in output — put everything inside brackets:

* [Open the door]
  The door creaks open, revealing a dusty hallway.

The player sees "Open the door" as a choice, but after clicking, only "The door creaks open..." appears in the story text.

Once-Only vs Sticky Choices

By default, choices marked with * are once-only — they disappear after being chosen. This is perfect for conversations where repeating the same line would feel wrong. But what happens when all choices vanish?

=== tavern ===
The barkeep polishes a glass.
* "Any news?" you ask.
  "Bandits on the north road," she says. -> tavern
* "One more ale."
  She fills your mug without a word. -> tavern
* -> tavern_end

=== tavern_end ===
You've exhausted your questions. Time to move on.
-> END

That last entry — * -> tavern_end with no display text — is a fallback choice. It's invisible to the player and triggers automatically when no other options remain. Without it, you'd get a runtime error ("ran out of content") once both real choices are used.

For situations where choices should persist — a menu, a browseable location — use + instead of *:

=== market ===
Stalls line the square.
+ [Browse weapons]
  The blacksmith shows you a fine blade. -> market
+ [Browse potions]
  Glass bottles glow on the shelf. -> market
* [Leave the market]
  You've seen enough. -> END

The + choices stay visible no matter how many times they're picked. The single * choice ("Leave the market") disappears after use — which is fine since it exits the loop. This sticky + once-only combination is the standard pattern for explorable spaces.

Stitches: Rooms Inside Rooms

In lesson 01, each knot was a self-contained location. But what happens when a knot gets long — say, a market with three stalls? You could make each stall its own knot, but then you need the full address everywhere. Stitches solve this — they're sub-sections within a knot, marked with a single =:

=== market_square ===
The market bustles with traders.
+ [Weapons stall] -> weapons
+ [Potion stall] -> potions

= weapons
"Looking for steel?" the blacksmith grins.
* "Show me your swords[."]," you say.
  He unsheathes a curved blade.
- You step back into the square.
-> market_square

= potions
Glass bottles line the stall.
* ["What's the blue one?"]
  "Restores stamina," the merchant says.
- You turn away.
-> market_square

Notice: inside market_square, you divert to -> weapons without the knot prefix. These are local diverts — they work because stitches live within their parent knot. From outside, you'd write -> market_square.weapons (the full address).

Think of it as extending the "rooms and pipes" metaphor from lesson 01: knots are buildings, stitches are rooms within a building. You can move between rooms without stepping outside.

Gathers: Where Branches Rejoin

So far, every time branches needed to reconverge, we used an explicit divert to another knot or stitch. That works, but it scatters the convergence point away from the divergence. The gather (-) solves this — it brings branches back together right where you are:

"What's that?" my master asked.
* "I am somewhat tired[."]," I repeated.
  "Really," he responded. "How deleterious."
* "Nothing, Monsieur!"[] I replied.
  "Very good, then."
* "I said, this journey is appalling[."]."
  "Ah," he replied. "I see you are frustrated."

- With that Monsieur Fogg left the room.

Regardless of which option the player chose, flow reaches the - gather and continues with "With that Monsieur Fogg left the room." No knot needed, no divert needed — the structure itself guarantees convergence.

This is the core of ink's weave philosophy: choices diverge, gathers converge, and flow always moves downward. You cannot create a broken link or an unreachable dead end within a weave — the structure prevents it.

Weave: Choices + Gathers Together

A single choice-and-gather pair is a building block. Chain them together for multi-beat conversations where each beat diverges briefly then reconverges before the next:

=== tavern_conversation ===
The barkeep catches your eye.

* [Sit at the bar]
  You slide onto a worn stool.
* [Find a quiet corner]
  You duck behind a pillar.

- "What'll it be?" she asks.

* "Ale[."]," you say.
  She pours a foaming mug.
* "Water[."], please."
  "Smart traveler," she says.

- You drink in silence, watching the room.
-> END

Two choice-gather pairs in sequence: where you sit, then what you drink. Each gather resets the playing field for the next beat. This is how 80 Days — Inkle's 600,000-word game — was written: "almost exclusively in weave." The pattern scales because each beat is self-contained.

When to use gathers vs diverts: Use gathers when branches are local and cosmetic — the player picked a flavor of the same moment. Use diverts (to knots/stitches) when the choice sends the player to a fundamentally different scene. Rule of thumb: if the branches rejoin within a few lines, use a gather.

Nested Choices (and When to Stop)

Sometimes a choice leads to a follow-up question. Nested choices use additional * characters — ** for a sub-choice, *** for a sub-sub-choice:

* "Do you have maps of the northern pass?"
  "Indeed!" He unfurls a parchment.
  * * [Buy the map]
      "A wise purchase."
  * * [Too expensive]
      "Perhaps next time," he says.
  -- You thank him and leave.
* "Any news from the road?"
  "Bandits near the bridge," he whispers.

- You leave the stall with plenty to think about.

The -- is a nested gather — it reconverges the sub-choices before flow continues to the outer - gather. Think of it as indentation: ** choices and -- gathers operate one level deeper than * and -.

In practice, 2-3 nesting levels is the maximum before the code becomes hard to read. If a sub-choice grows complex, escape to a stitch:

* "Tell me about the map." -> map_details

= map_details
  "It shows three routes through the pass."
  * * [Ask about the mountain route]
      "Steep but fast."
  * * [Ask about the valley route]
      "Longer, but safer."
  -- "Either way, bring warm clothes."
  -> market_square

Diverting to a stitch when nesting gets deep is idiomatic ink — it keeps each section readable while maintaining the organizational benefit of stitches within a knot.

The market square bustles with traders and travelers. Three stalls catch your eye.

+ [Browse the potion stall] -> market_square.potions

- You step back into the square.
-> market_square

= potions
Glass bottles line the stall, filled with luminous liquids.

* ["What's the blue one?"]
  "Restores stamina," the merchant says. "Five coins."
* ["What's the red one?"]
  "Heals wounds. Ten coins — worth every copper."
* ["I'll pass for now."]
  The merchant nods, unbothered.

- The colors swirl as you turn away.
-> market_square

= maps
An old cartographer peers at you over half-moon spectacles.

* "Do you have maps of the northern pass?"
  "Indeed!" He unfurls a parchment.
  * * [Buy the map for three coins]
      "A wise purchase." He rolls it up neatly for you.
  * * [It's too expensive]
      "Perhaps next time," he says, folding it away.
  -- You thank him for his time.
* "Any news from the road[?"]?" you ask.
  "Bandits near the bridge," he whispers. "Take the forest path."

- You leave the stall with plenty to think about.
-> market_square

=== tavern ===
The tavern is warm and loud. A bard plays in the corner.

* [Sit at the bar]
  You slide onto a stool, the wood worn smooth by years of use.
* [Find a quiet corner]
  You duck behind a pillar, away from the noise.

- The barkeep catches your eye. "What'll it be?"

* "Ale[."]," you say.
  She pours a foaming mug and slides it across.
* "Water[."], please."
  "Smart traveler," she says, filling a clay cup.
* "Information[."]," you say quietly.
  She leans in. "That depends on the coin."

- You drink in silence, watching the room.

* "Who's the bard?" you ask the barkeep.
  "Calls herself Wren. Been here a week."
* "Any rooms available?"
  "Last one on the left, upstairs. Two coins a night."
* -> tavern_end

- -> tavern

= tavern_end
The candle on your table gutters low. Time to decide.

* [Head upstairs to rest] -> rest
* [Slip out into the night] -> night_road

=== rest ===
You climb the narrow stairs and find your room. The bed is simple but clean.
Tomorrow, the northern pass awaits.
-> END

=== night_road ===
The cobblestones are slick with evening rain. The forest path the cartographer mentioned — it begins just past the bridge.
You pull your cloak tight and walk into the dark.
-> END

Open this in Inky and play through multiple times. Notice how the sticky choices (+) in the market let you visit all three stalls, while the once-only choices (*) in the tavern conversation build toward the fallback exit. The gathers (-) inside each stitch bring you back to the market hub seamlessly.

Check Your Understanding

Exercise: Write an ink scene with one knot containing two stitches. The main knot offers sticky choices to visit each stitch. Each stitch should have a short dialogue using bracket syntax and a gather that returns to the main knot. Add a once-only choice that exits the loop (appearing after at least one stitch is visited). Verify in Inky that you can visit both stitches and then leave.

Hint

Structure: one === knot === with + choices pointing to = stitch_a and = stitch_b. Each stitch uses * choices with [brackets], a - gather, then -> knot to return. A final * choice exits with -> END.

Answer

Any hub-and-spoke structure works. Here's a minimal example:

=== garden ===
The garden has two paths.
+ [Visit the fountain] -> fountain
+ [Visit the roses] -> roses
* [Leave the garden] -> END

= fountain
Water splashes in the stone basin.
* "Beautiful[."]," you murmur.
* [Toss a coin in]
  It sinks with a satisfying plop.
- You walk back to the main path.
-> garden

= roses
Red blooms climb a wooden trellis.
* [Smell the roses]
  Sweet and heady.
* "These would make a fine gift[."]," you think.
- You return to the crossroads.
-> garden

The key insight: sticky choices create explorable spaces; gathers create local convergence; stitches organize sub-locations without needing separate knots. This hub-and-spoke pattern — a main loop with stitch spokes — is how ink handles explorable environments.
