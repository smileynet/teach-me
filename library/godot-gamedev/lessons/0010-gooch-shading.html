Gooch Warm/Cool Shadows

Lesson 10 · Per-Material Toon Shading (MKToon) · ~8 min read
Win: You can implement Gooch shading and explain why tinting shadows cool creates perceived depth — and why the dark_color must have non-zero channels.
After this lesson: Your toon shader replaces flat dark shadows with a cool-to-warm color ramp. Three new uniforms give per-material control over shadow and highlight tinting — the single highest-impact quality upgrade for any cel shader.

The Problem: Black Shadows Look Amateur

← Previous: Lesson 9: Configurable Banding

Your configurable banding shader from Lesson 9 produces clean discrete bands — but the shadow side is just darker albedo. At the lowest band, the surface approaches black. This is the single most common "student shader" tell: physically, shadowed surfaces still receive ambient light, reflected bounce, and atmospheric color. Pure black shadows scream "this was rendered by a computer."

Professional toon shading — from Studio Ghibli to Genshin Impact — uses colored shadows. Specifically: cool-tinted shadows (blue, purple, blue-gray) and warm-tinted highlights (cream, yellow, warm white). This creates depth through color temperature rather than just brightness.

Why Warm/Cool Creates Depth

Three reinforcing mechanisms make this work:

  1. Color temperature perception: Warm colors (red, orange, yellow) perceptually advance toward the viewer. Cool colors (blue, purple) perceptually recede. Putting warm on lit surfaces and cool on shadows amplifies the sense of volume.
  2. Outdoor lighting physics: In real outdoor scenes, the key light (sun) is warm (~5000K), while fill light (blue sky) is cool (~10000K). Shadows naturally take on the fill color. Colored shadows are more physically accurate than black ones.
  3. Illustration tradition: Gouache, watercolor, and animation cel painting all use this principle. Learners intuitively recognize "painted quality" even if they can't articulate why.

This technique was formalized by Gooch et al. in 1998 as a non-photorealistic lighting model for technical illustration. We use a simplified "one-sided" version: shadows get a cool tint; lit areas stay at natural albedo.

Implementation: Three Uniforms, Three Lines

We add three new uniforms to our configurable banding shader:

// === Gooch Shading ===
uniform vec4 gooch_bright_color : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform vec4 gooch_dark_color : source_color = vec4(0.2, 0.25, 0.35, 1.0);
uniform float gooch_ramp_intensity : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;

And replace the final color line in light(). Previously we had:

// OLD: scalar multiply (shadows are just dark albedo)
DIFFUSE_LIGHT = max(DIFFUSE_LIGHT, ALBEDO * LIGHT_COLOR * ATTENUATION * lit_factor);

The new version computes tinted endpoints and interpolates between them:

// Gooch shading: tint shadow and lit endpoints, then interpolate by lit_factor
vec3 shadow_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_dark_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
vec3 lit_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_bright_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
vec3 toon_color = mix(shadow_tint, lit_tint, lit_factor);

DIFFUSE_LIGHT = max(DIFFUSE_LIGHT, toon_color * LIGHT_COLOR * ATTENUATION);

Notice: lit_factor is no longer multiplied against ALBEDO directly. Instead, it selects between two pre-tinted versions of the albedo. The banding result drives a color ramp rather than a brightness ramp.

Why Multiply (Not Replace)

Both endpoints are ALBEDO * gooch_color, not just gooch_color. This is deliberate:

  • A red cloth with dark_color = blue-gray → shadows become dark desaturated red-blue (not pure blue)
  • A green tree with dark_color = blue-gray → shadows become dark teal-green (not pure blue)
  • The object's base color always shows through — Gooch tints, never replaces

If you used mix(gooch_dark_color.rgb, gooch_bright_color.rgb, lit_factor) without ALBEDO, every object would look the same in shadow regardless of its base color. The multiply preserves material identity.

The Intensity Dial

The gooch_ramp_intensity uniform blends between "no Gooch" and "full Gooch":

  • intensity = 0.0 → shadow_tint = ALBEDO, lit_tint = ALBEDO → identical to Lesson 9 (scalar lighting)
  • intensity = 0.5 → halfway between scalar and full color shift
  • intensity = 1.0 → shadows fully tinted by dark_color, lit fully tinted by bright_color
When to use which:
  • High intensity (0.7–1.0) — cloth, skin, organic materials. These benefit from strong color temperature contrast.
  • Low intensity (0.1–0.3) — metals, glass, dark materials. Too much Gooch on metallics looks wrong because real metal shadows are their own reflected color.
  • Per-material control is the point. One material's ideal Gooch is another's worst case. This is why we build it as a uniform rather than a global post-process.

The White Bright-Color Shortcut

With the default gooch_bright_color = white, the lit endpoint simplifies:

lit_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * vec3(1.0), gooch_ramp_intensity)
         = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO, gooch_ramp_intensity)
         = ALBEDO  // always, regardless of intensity

So lit areas are always the natural albedo. Only shadows get the cool tint. This is "one-sided Gooch" — simpler than the full academic version, and what most shipped games use. If you later want warm highlights (golden lit areas), change gooch_bright_color to a warm cream like vec4(1.0, 0.95, 0.85, 1.0).

The Complete Shader

Here's the full shader with all banding controls from Lesson 9 plus the three Gooch uniforms. The only change in light() is the final four lines — everything above lit_factor is identical to Lesson 9:

shader_type spatial;
render_mode specular_disabled;

// === Albedo ===
uniform vec4 albedo_color : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform sampler2D albedo_texture : source_color;
uniform bool use_albedo_texture = false;

// === Toon Banding Controls ===
uniform float light_bands : hint_range(1, 9, 1) = 4.0;
uniform float light_bands_scale : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;
uniform float light_threshold : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;
uniform float diffuse_threshold_offset : hint_range(-1, 1) = 0.0;
uniform float diffuse_smoothness : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.0;

// === Gooch Shading ===
uniform vec4 gooch_bright_color : source_color = vec4(1.0);
uniform vec4 gooch_dark_color : source_color = vec4(0.2, 0.25, 0.35, 1.0);
uniform float gooch_ramp_intensity : hint_range(0, 1) = 0.5;

void fragment() {
    vec3 col = albedo_color.rgb;
    if (use_albedo_texture) {
        col *= texture(albedo_texture, UV).rgb;
    }
    ALBEDO = col;
}

void light() {
    float NdotL = dot(NORMAL, LIGHT);

    // Center around threshold (Half Lambert remap)
    float centered = NdotL - light_threshold - diffuse_threshold_offset + 0.5;
    centered = clamp(centered, 0.0, 1.0);

    // Band quantization
    float banded = floor(centered * light_bands) / max(light_bands - 1.0, 1.0);

    // Smoothness blend
    float lit_factor = mix(banded, centered, diffuse_smoothness);

    // Scale
    lit_factor = 0.5 + (lit_factor - 0.5) * light_bands_scale;

    // Gooch shading: tint shadow and lit endpoints, then interpolate by lit_factor
    vec3 shadow_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_dark_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
    vec3 lit_tint = mix(ALBEDO, ALBEDO * gooch_bright_color.rgb, gooch_ramp_intensity);
    vec3 toon_color = mix(shadow_tint, lit_tint, lit_factor);

    DIFFUSE_LIGHT = max(DIFFUSE_LIGHT, toon_color * LIGHT_COLOR * ATTENUATION);
}

Check Your Understanding

Exercise: A colleague adds Gooch shading to their toon shader but sets gooch_dark_color = vec4(0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0) — pure black — expecting "really dark cool shadows." They report that the shadow areas look identical to the version without Gooch. "It's not doing anything," they say. Explain why black doesn't work and what they should set instead.

Hint

Look at the formula: ALBEDO * gooch_dark_color.rgb. What happens when you multiply any color by vec3(0, 0, 0)?

Answer

ALBEDO * vec3(0, 0, 0) = vec3(0, 0, 0) — black. And mix(ALBEDO, vec3(0, 0, 0), intensity) just darkens toward black — which is exactly what the original scalar lighting did. Black as a tint color produces the same result as no Gooch at all.

The dark_color needs actual color channels to create a visible tint shift. Set it to something like vec4(0.2, 0.25, 0.4, 1.0) (cool blue-gray) or vec4(0.3, 0.15, 0.35, 1.0) (purple). The non-zero channels are what make shadows look like they're receiving colored ambient light rather than just getting darker.

This is the core Gooch insight: shadows aren't the absence of light — they're the presence of a different color of light.
