Emit Bake & glTF Export — Blender to Godot

Lesson 19 · Blender to Godot Texture Prep · ~15 min read
Win: You can bake your posterized + palette-snapped albedo with a Cycles Emit pass, export a toon-ready glTF, and explain why Emit (not Combined) and why the control maps stay out of the glTF.
After this lesson: The Emit bake captures material color before lighting touches it — the only bake type safe for dynamic toon shading. Everything else freezes shadows into the texture and fights the shader.

The Problem

You've built the whole prep chain: posterize the albedo (lesson 16), snap it to a palette (17), author control maps (18). It all lives in Blender's node editor. Now you need it in Godot — as texture files and a mesh the engine imports. This lesson is the round-trip: bake the node graph down to flat textures, export a glTF, and import it cleanly with correct color spaces.

Two decisions make or break it: which bake type, and what goes in the glTF. Both have a wrong answer that looks fine in Blender and breaks in Godot.

Why Emit, not Combined

A bake captures a material's output to a texture. But which output? A Combined or Diffuse bake includes scene lighting — the shadows and highlights from your Blender lights get painted into the texture. That's fine for a statically-lit asset. It's a disaster for toon shading.

Godot's toon shader computes lighting dynamically — it bands the light in real time. If your albedo already has baked shadows, you get double shadows: the baked-in ones plus the live-computed ones, fighting each other. The texture looks great in Blender's viewport and terrible in Godot.

Emit bakes what the material emits — its raw color output, with zero lighting influence. To use it, wire your prep chain into an Emission shader instead of the Principled BSDF, then bake type = "EMIT". The result is pure prepped color, ready for the shader to light however it wants.

Why Cycles and not EEVEE? EEVEE can't bake at all (as of Blender 4.x/5.x). More subtly: EEVEE's Shader to RGB node — a common toon trick — doesn't work in a Cycles bake. That's exactly why lessons 16–17 built posterize/palette-snap from plain Math and Vector nodes: they're Cycles-bakeable, so the whole chain survives the Emit bake.

Baking the barrel

The script chains lesson 16's Posterize RGB group and lesson 17's Palette Snap group into an Emission shader, then Emit-bakes the result to a 1K sRGB image. It reuses those groups directly — importing them, not re-implementing:

import bpy, importlib.util
from pathlib import Path

def _load(path, name):
    spec = importlib.util.spec_from_file_location(name, str(path))
    m = importlib.util.module_from_spec(spec)
    spec.loader.exec_module(m)
    return m

posterize = _load(POSTERIZE_PY, "posterize_rgb")
palette   = _load(PALETTE_PY, "palette_snap")

def build_bake_material(src_image):
    posterize.build_group(); palette.build_all()   # rebuild AFTER any factory reset
    mat = bpy.data.materials.new("ToonPrepBake"); mat.use_nodes = True
    nt = mat.node_tree; nt.nodes.clear()
    tex = nt.nodes.new("ShaderNodeTexImage"); tex.image = src_image
    p = nt.nodes.new("ShaderNodeGroup"); p.node_tree = bpy.data.node_groups["Posterize RGB"]
    s = nt.nodes.new("ShaderNodeGroup"); s.node_tree = bpy.data.node_groups[palette.GROUP_B]
    emit = nt.nodes.new("ShaderNodeEmission")            # <-- Emission, NOT Principled
    out  = nt.nodes.new("ShaderNodeOutputMaterial")
    nt.links.new(tex.outputs["Color"], p.inputs["Color"])
    nt.links.new(p.outputs["Color"], s.inputs["Color"])
    nt.links.new(s.outputs["Color"], emit.inputs["Color"])
    nt.links.new(emit.outputs["Emission"], out.inputs["Surface"])
    return mat

The baked result is the same toon-prepped albedo you saw snapped in lesson 17 — now frozen into a flat texture with no lighting in it.

Multi-material assets (e.g. Camera_01). An asset with several materials (the camera has a strap, a lens body, and a glass lens) bakes each material separately — one Emit bake per material slot, to its own texture or an atlas. Watch for materials with no albedo texture: the camera's lens is a glass/transmission material with only a base-color factor, so there's nothing to posterize — you handle it as a solid color, not a bake.

Exporting the glTF — and what stays out of it

Export the mesh + baked albedo as a .glb. The export settings exclude what Godot doesn't need — lights and cameras (Godot lights the scene itself), and use PNG textures (not JPEG — lossy compression adds the noise back you just removed):

bpy.ops.export_scene.gltf(
    filepath="Barrel_01_toon.glb",
    export_format="GLB",
    export_materials="EXPORT",
    export_cameras=False,      # Godot's toon shader lights dynamically —
    export_lights=False,       # a baked light would double-shadow
    use_selection=True,        # just the barrel, no stray objects
)

Now the critical decision: the control maps do NOT go in the glTF. Here's why.

Gotcha — glTF color space is slot-driven. glTF has no per-image "this is sRGB / this is data" flag. Instead, Godot infers color space from which material slot a texture sits in: a texture in baseColorTexture is read as sRGB; one in normalTexture as linear. That's why the baked albedo imports correctly with zero fuss — it's color data in the base-color slot.

But a control map (noise, threshold) is data, not color, and it has no correct glTF slot. Put it in baseColorTexture and Godot sRGB-decodes it, corrupting the control values. Worse: a texture embedded in a .glb has no separate .import file, so you can't fix its color space after import. The fix is to keep the control maps out of the glTF entirely — ship them as standalone Non-Color PNGs and wire them into the Godot material by hand. The glTF carries only the albedo.

Importing into Godot

Drop the .glb into your Godot project. On import, Godot flags the albedo texture sRGB automatically (it's in the base-color slot). The control-map PNGs, imported as separate files, get their own import settings — set those to Non-Color so their data isn't gamma-shifted. Then assign all three to the toon material's texture slots.

Check Your Understanding

Exercise: A colleague bakes their prepped albedo with the Combined pass (Direct lighting enabled). It looks perfect in Blender's viewport. In Godot, under the toon shader, the barrel looks muddy and the shadows are weirdly dark and doubled. They ask you what went wrong and which bake type to use instead. What do you tell them?

Hint

What does a Combined bake capture that an Emit bake doesn't? And what does Godot's toon shader do to lighting that Blender's baked texture already did?

Answer

What went wrong: the Combined pass baked Blender's scene lighting — shadows and highlights — into the albedo texture. Godot's toon shader then computes its own dynamic lighting on top of that already-lit texture. So every shadow is applied twice: once frozen in the texture, once live from the shader. That's the "doubled, weirdly dark" look. It's fine in Blender because Blender isn't re-lighting the baked result; Godot is.

The fix: use an Emit bake (wire the prep chain into an Emission shader, bake type="EMIT"). Emit captures the material's color output with no lighting influence, so Godot's shader is the only thing lighting the surface — no double shadows. (Equivalently: a Diffuse bake with both Direct and Indirect contributions unchecked.) The rule: for dynamic toon shading, the baked albedo must be unlit.
